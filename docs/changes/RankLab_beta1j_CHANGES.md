# RankLab beta 1j: changes (15 November 2026)

Round 16. Built from beta 1i by exact replacements (`verify/round16/make_ranklab_1j.py`; a replacement that does not match exactly once stops the build). 5,093 lines (beta 1i: 3,845); 127 routines (107), of which 20 are new and 37 changed (`verify/round16/linemap_r16.py` lists them).

## Why

Two tests more, and three faults found on the way.

1. **Page's L, a test for an ordered trend.** Roger, 15 November 2026, had a book of tables checked against fresh calculations (Zwillinger & Kokoska, 2000; the report is `claude/CRC_table_check_2026-11-15.md` in the Project). Its second suggestion for our programs was "Page's L test in RankLab. It is the test in Chapter 9's story. Its exact null distribution is a short convolution". Friedman's test asks whether the treatments differ at all; Page's asks whether they rise (or fall) in an order named beforehand, which is the sharper question when there is such an order.
2. **A runs test for randomness.** The third suggestion: "Triola teaches it and the fleet has none." Roger, the same day: "interesting to refer back to the HP-67 programs if possible". Hewlett-Packard's own "Run Test for Randomness" of 1975 has an example worth keeping, a statistician at a roulette table, and it is now a preset.
3. **"infinite" where a number belonged.** Found by this round's checks with larger data: the Null page wrote "infinite" for any statistic of 100,000 or more (Mann–Whitney's U with two groups of 500), and its read-out then started at the window's edge. The word is for Spearman's t of a perfect order only. Old code.
4. **An axis number off the screen.** The Null page's last axis number ran past the right edge for a statistic in the hundred thousands. Old code.
5. **A table too big for one list.** "Your data" with more than 10,000 values in a blocks table (3 columns of 3,400 rows) went past what one list holds. It is now refused with a sentence.

Of the six suggestions Roger said: "Add all please."

**What was read.** For the runs test, Hewlett-Packard's keystroke programs: the HP-65 Stat Pac 2 (1975), program Stat 2-22A, "The Run Test for Randomness", with its example on p. 86; the HP-67/HP-97 Users' Library Solutions book *Test Statistics*, pp. 27 to 31, which calls its program a translation of the HP-65's; and the HP-41C's RUNTEST. They take the two counts and the number of runs, keyed in, and return the mean, the standard deviation and z; for small samples they send the user to printed tables. Their pages were read to see what the programs compute. RankLab counts the runs from the sequence itself and adds the exact p and the pictures; it is written new in PPL from the formulas, and none of their code is in it. For Page's L no program was read: Page (1963), Hollander, Wolfe & Chicken (2014, section 7.2) and the notes of SciPy's `page_trend_test`. The library in `D:\HP-Prime` holds no program with Page's L and, beyond those HP keystroke programs, no runs test (searched 15 November 2026).

**Page's L with ties.** The variance RankLab uses is the exact one for the data's own ties: the sum over blocks of k(k + 1)(k(k² − 1) − T_i)/144, where T_i is the sum of t³ − t over the block's tied groups. Its exact p is counted over every order of each block's own midranks, so it is the p conditional on the ties (Hollander, Wolfe & Chicken, section 7.2, Comment 19). R's `PageTest` and SciPy's `method='exact'` use the untied null whatever the data, and with ties their normal approximation keeps the untied variance, which Comment 21 calls conservative. So the three can differ, and the ozone preset shows it: L = 48, RankLab's exact p is 14/27 = 0.5185, and the untied null gives 83/144 = 0.5764.

**The runs test's formulas.** With n₁ and n₂ of the two kinds and n = n₁ + n₂: the mean is 2n₁n₂/n + 1 and the variance 2n₁n₂(2n₁n₂ − n)/(n²(n − 1)). The number of orders with 2k runs is 2·C(n₁ − 1, k − 1)·C(n₂ − 1, k − 1), and with 2k + 1 runs that number times (n − 2k)/(2k); all C(n, n₁) orders are equally likely under H₀. The two-sided exact p is twice the smaller tail, at most 1 (RankLab's rule for every test). z carries no continuity correction, as the HP-65 program and the texts print it.

**The presets' numbers** (each from exact arithmetic in Python; `verify_RankLab_beta1j.py`, P0 to P12):

- *Ozone* (4 subjects at 0.1, 0.6 and 1.0 ppm; Goldsmith & Nadel, 1969, as given by Hollander, Wolfe & Chicken, 2014, Table 7.2): L = 48, mean 48, SD 2.74 with the tie; exact p = 0.5185 (rising); z = 0, normal p = 0.5000.
- *Draft 1970, months in order* (days 1 to 28 as blocks, the twelve months in calendar order): L = 13,440, mean 14,196, SD 228.15; z = −3.314, p = 0.0005 (falling). Twelve treatments are too many for the exact count.
- *Roulette* (HP-65 Stat Pac 2: 25 spins, 14 red and 11 black, 9 runs): mean 13.32, SD 2.41, z = −1.792, as the HP book prints them (13.32, 2.41, −1.79); exact two-sided p = 0.1101 (the lower tail is 409/7,429 = 0.0551); normal two-sided p = 0.0731.
- *Draft 1970, runs by day* (above or below the median number, n₁ = n₂ = 183): 172 runs, mean 184.00, SD 9.55; exact two-sided p = 0.2286; z = −1.256, normal p = 0.2090; the longest run is 12 below, places 355 to 366.

**One lottery, two answers.** The runs test by day sees nothing in the 1970 draft (p = 0.23); Page's L by month does (p = 0.0005), as the rank correlation of day with number does. The order of the days was not what went wrong; the later months drew the lower numbers. A runs test looks for clusters and alternation in the order, and misses a slow drift that leaves the runs alone.
